# Words: what reads generated, and what to write instead

The test: **would the owner say it out loud to a customer at the counter?** If not, rewrite.

## Words with reasons

slop_check.py fails on these. The reason matters more than the list: each one is what a
writer reaches for when they have nothing specific to say.

- **Agency words:** elevate, unlock, empower, unleash, supercharge, leverage, seamless,
  robust, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, game-changer, next-level, synergy, holistic,
  revolutionize. Say what actually changes for the reader.
- **Brochure words:** nestled, vibrant, bustling, hidden gem, breathtaking, paradise,
  crystal-clear. Name the place, the time, the thing you'll see.
- **Generator tics:** delve, tapestry, testament to, realm, embark, journey, navigate the,
  symphony of, in today's fast-paced world, whether you're X or Y, look no further.
- **Food clichés:** mouthwatering, delectable, scrumptious, tantalizing, indulge, savor,
  culinary, gourmet, artisan, perfection, bursting with flavor. Name the ingredient and
  how it's cooked.
- **Claims of care:** passionate, crafted, handcrafted, meticulous, curated, with love.
  Show the care: who makes it, how, how long.
- **Placeholders:** lorem ipsum, John Doe, Acme, "Sarah M., California".

Watch (not banned, but they pile up): amazing, incredible, stunning, delicious,
unforgettable, authentic, fresh, simply, effortlessly, exclamation marks.

## Rhythms

- **Staccato triads:** "Relax. Unwind. Experience." / "Fresh. Bold. Unforgettable." The
  most recognisable generated rhythm there is. Say it once, plainly.
- **"Not just X, it's Y"** / "more than just a boat trip". Just say what it is.
- **Rhetorical-question openers:** "Ready to set sail?", "Looking for the perfect…?"
  The reader didn't ask. Tell them what they need.
- **Brochure verb + "the/your":** "Discover the…", "Experience the…", "Elevate your…".
- **The three-adjective stack:** "a fun, relaxing and unforgettable experience". Pick the
  one that's true and prove it with a detail.
- **Em dashes:** nobody types one in a chat or writes one on a menu board. Use a full
  stop, a comma, or two sentences.
- **No contractions:** "We are open", "You will love it". It reads like a form letter.
- **Title Case Headings And Buttons:** sentence case reads like a person wrote it.

## Buttons and headings

- Buttons say what happens next: "Book on WhatsApp", "Send my order", "See the menu".
  Not "Learn more", "Get started", "Submit", "Click here".
- Section names say what's in the section: "What's included", "Where we leave from",
  "Prices". Not "Why choose us", "Our features", "What our customers say", "Our story".

## Proof

Never write a review, a rating, a customer count, a founding year, an award or a name
nobody gave you. If there are no reviews yet, there's no reviews section. If the owner
gives a real one, quote it exactly, with their OK, with a first name and where it came
from ("Ana, on WhatsApp"). Real numbers are the best proof there is: "Max 12 guests",
"2.5 hours", "US$85 including drinks".

## Messages (welcome emails, notifications, posts)

- **One job.** Decide the single thing the reader should do or know after reading. Lead
  with it: "If you only do one thing today, snap a receipt."
- **Then stop.** Two or three supporting lines at most. Every other true detail lives in
  the product, where it's needed. A feature tour with every number in it (rates, limits,
  formats, tax percentages) reads like a brochure, even when nothing in it is false.
- **Sound like the person who sends it.** First person, a real name, a reply that reaches a
  real inbox, an honest line ("you're one of the first businesses on it").
- **Under about 150 words** for most messages. If it needs more, it's probably two messages.

## Writing for a place

Local beats global. Name the street, the beach, the neighbourhood, the time it gets dark.
Use the local language where people really use it (a greeting, a dish name), but don't
machine-translate whole pages into a language nobody on the team checks.
